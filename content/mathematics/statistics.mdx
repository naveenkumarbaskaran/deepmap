## Why Statistics?

Probability tells us how to reason about random processes. Statistics flips the question: given observed data, what can we infer about the underlying process? In ML, this means estimating model parameters, diagnosing overfitting, and deciding whether a result is real or noise.

## Descriptive Statistics

Before modeling, describe your data:

- **Mean** $\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i$ — central tendency; sensitive to outliers
- **Median** — middle value; robust to outliers
- **Variance** $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2$ — spread around the mean
- **Standard deviation** $\sigma$ — same units as the data; easier to interpret
- **Skewness** — asymmetry of the distribution (long left or right tail)
- **Kurtosis** — weight in the tails (heavy-tailed vs light-tailed)

For ML: always check these before training. High variance features may need normalization; skewed targets may need log transformation.

## Covariance and Correlation

**Covariance** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]$ measures how two variables move together. Positive means they rise together; negative means one rises as the other falls.

**Pearson correlation** $\rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \cdot \sigma_Y}$ normalizes covariance to $[-1, 1]$. It measures *linear* association only. The correlation matrix across all features reveals redundancy (high $\rho$ = multicollinearity) and informs feature selection.

## The Central Limit Theorem

The CLT states: if you draw $n$ independent samples from *any* distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, their sample mean $\bar{X}$ approaches a Gaussian distribution as $n \to \infty$:

$$\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$$

This is why the Gaussian distribution is ubiquitous in ML: loss values, weight updates, and many model outputs are sums of many small independent contributions, so they approximately follow a Gaussian regardless of the original distribution.

## Hypothesis Testing

A **hypothesis test** decides whether observed data provides enough evidence to reject a null hypothesis $H_0$. Key concepts:

- **p-value**: probability of seeing data this extreme if $H_0$ were true. Low p-value → reject $H_0$.
- **Type I error** ($\alpha$): false positive — rejecting $H_0$ when it's true
- **Type II error** ($\beta$): false negative — failing to reject a false $H_0$
- **Statistical power** ($1-\beta$): probability of detecting a true effect

In ML, A/B testing a new model uses these exact ideas: is the accuracy improvement real, or sampling noise?

## Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The expected test error of any estimator decomposes as:

$$\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{y})^2] = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible noise}$$

- **Bias**: error from wrong assumptions — underfitting. A linear model on nonlinear data has high bias.
- **Variance**: error from sensitivity to training data fluctuations — overfitting. A depth-20 decision tree memorizes training noise.

The tradeoff: reducing bias often increases variance and vice versa. Regularization, ensemble methods, and early stopping are all strategies to navigate this tradeoff.

```python runnable
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
data = rng.normal(5.0, 2.0, 500)

# Descriptive statistics
print("=== Descriptive Stats ===")
print(f"Mean:     {data.mean():.4f}")
print(f"Std:      {data.std():.4f}")
print(f"Variance: {data.var():.4f}")
print(f"Min/Max:  {data.min():.2f} / {data.max():.2f}")

# Central Limit Theorem demo
n_samples, sample_size = 1000, 30
# Original distribution: heavily skewed (exponential)
population = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=50000)
print(f"\n=== CLT Demo ===")
print(f"Population: Exponential(2). Mean={population.mean():.3f}, Std={population.std():.3f}")

sample_means = [rng.choice(population, sample_size).mean() for _ in range(n_samples)]
sample_means = np.array(sample_means)
print(f"Sample means (n={sample_size}): Mean={sample_means.mean():.3f}, Std={sample_means.std():.3f}")
print(f"CLT predicted std: {population.std() / np.sqrt(sample_size):.3f}")

# Correlation matrix
x = rng.normal(0, 1, 200)
y = 0.8 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.6, 200)   # correlated
z = rng.normal(0, 1, 200)                # independent

mat = np.column_stack([x, y, z])
corr = np.corrcoef(mat.T)
print(f"\n=== Correlation Matrix ===")
print(np.round(corr, 2))
```
